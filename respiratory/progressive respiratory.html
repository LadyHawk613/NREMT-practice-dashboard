Phase 5 – Debrief & Discussion Questions

Click a question below to view the clinical discussion points:

1. Why can decreasing wheezing represent worsening asthma? +
Wheezing requires moving air through narrowed airways. When smooth muscle constriction and mucus plugging become severe enough to reduce air movement to near zero ("silent chest"), wheezing stops because there is insufficient airflow to produce sound.
2. Why is exhaustion a dangerous sign in severe asthma? +
Breathing through constricted airways exponentially increases the work of breathing. When respiratory muscles fatigue, respiratory rate and tidal volume drop sharply, leading rapidly to respiratory failure and arrest.
3. At what point does oxygen therapy become ventilation? +
Supplemental oxygen is insufficient when the patient cannot adequately move volume (inadequate tidal volume, bradypnea, or severe fatigue). Once ventilation fails to maintain air movement, positive-pressure ventilation (BVM) is required.
4. Why is asthma primarily a ventilation problem? +
Asthma involves mechanical bronchospasm, edema, and mucus plugging that physically impede air moving in and out of the alveoli. Supplying oxygen alone does not solve the physical restriction to air movement; volume movement (ventilation) must be supported.

Case #4: "Breathing Fast Isn't Always Anxiety"

Phase 5 – Debrief & Discussion Questions

Click a question below to view the clinical discussion points:

1. Why can pulmonary embolism present with clear lung sounds? +
Pulmonary embolism is a vascular occlusion (perfusion problem) in the pulmonary arteries, not an airway or alveolar fluid problem. Because the bronchial airways and alveoli remain clear of fluid or spasm, breath sounds usually remain clear.
2. Why does a normal early SpO₂ not rule out severe pathology? +
A patient can maintain near-normal arterial saturation initially by hyperventilating (increasing RR and minute volume). As compensatory mechanisms fail or the clot burden impacts hemodynamic stability, SpO₂ drops rapidly.
3. What historical clues strongly suggest pulmonary embolism? +
Recent prolonged immobilization (long air travel), unilateral leg swelling/tenderness (indicative of a Deep Vein Thrombosis), sudden onset dyspnea, and pleuritic (sharp, inhalation-dependent) chest pain.
4. Why is anchoring on an "anxiety" diagnosis dangerous? +
Anchoring bias leads clinicians to ignore critical red flags (pleuritic pain, hypoxia, tachycardia, DVT signs). Treating a physical emergency as psychological delays life-saving oxygenation, transport, and definitive care.
